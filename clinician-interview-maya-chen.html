How Dr Maya Chen uses AI and still makes the clinical call.

A practical look at where AI can help on a busy day, where it must stop, and why each answer needs a visible source.

Dr Maya ChenGeneral practitioner · complex record review
2026-Aug-24

What was record review like before n1?

Much of the work came before I could think about care. I would open a PDF, find the date, and copy a result into a sheet. Then I would do it again for years of files. One odd value could send me back through them all. I had to check if the unit, range, or health facts had changed.

The hard part was not one result. It was keeping the full chain in my head while I got ready for the person in front of me.

Where does AI help first?

I use it for the first pass. It can sort files by date, pull out the values, and put repeat tests on one line. That gives me a clear view before I start my review.

I do not want a sure claim that I cannot check. I want the value, the lab range, and the page it came from. If one is missing, I will not use the claim.

“The useful part is not that AI gives me an answer. It is that I can see what the answer was built from.”

How does this change a consultation?

If a patient asks why a test matters, I can show the trend. We can check it against the right range and open the source result at once.

That makes our conversation more direct. I spend less time on where a number came from and more time on what it may mean.

What do you still check?

I check all work that leaves n1. I check key data, read why each main claim was made, and edit the words. I then decide if the advice fits the full health picture. AI can draft a report. It cannot own the final copy.

I also look for gaps. A neat trend can still mislead us if care changed, the lab range moved, or a key file did not arrive. A good tool should make those gaps easy to see.

Where should AI stop?

It should stop before the care choice and before a report is shared. The doctor must control the words, sign-off, reader, and link. I want to know which copy the patient saw. I also want to be able to turn off that link.

What does a good day with AI feel like?

Quiet. The files are in order, odd values are easy to check, and each source is one click away. I reach the consultation with a draft I can challenge, not an answer I must trust.

That is the role I want AI to play: less clerical work, clear evidence, and more time for clinical judgment.
